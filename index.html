## Foundation brief — metacognition, from the primary literature

(Coordinator-side summary of the established literature, included so all lanes argue from the same base. A fuller research report with sources is being compiled in parallel; treat any point here you cannot verify as UNCONFIRMED and say so.)

- **Flavell (1979)** defined metacognition as knowledge and cognition about cognitive phenomena: *metacognitive knowledge* (about person, task and strategy variables) and *metacognitive experiences*, in service of monitoring one's own cognitive enterprises.
- **Nelson & Narens (1990)** formalised two relations between an object level and a meta level: **monitoring** (object → meta: ease-of-learning judgements, judgements of learning (JOLs), feeling-of-knowing, retrospective confidence) and **control** (meta → object: strategy selection, allocation of effort, termination of work on an item). A self-detection system is exactly a monitoring channel plus the control actions wired to it.
- **Monitoring is inferential, not a direct read-out** (Koriat's cue-utilisation framework): judgements are built from cues, and **processing fluency is a misleading cue** — material that feels fluent and familiar is judged better known/mastered than it is. A fluent, well-formatted artefact or report can therefore *feel* verified without having been verified.
- **Calibration is measurable** (confidence vs outcome frequency; calibration curves, Brier scores, resolution). Robust findings: (a) overconfidence is the default across most judgement domains; (b) the best-calibrated human judges (operational weather forecasters; Murphy & Winkler) reached calibration through **dense, immediate, unambiguous outcome feedback on many small recorded predictions**; (c) **postdictions calibrate better than predictions**, and **delayed JOLs are far more accurate than immediate JOLs** (Nelson & Dunlosky 1991) — monitoring improves with a temporal gap and with a retrieval attempt in between; (d) calibration training works when predictions are specific, recorded before the outcome, scored, and reviewed in aggregate (Tetlock's forecaster research programme).
- **The planning fallacy** (Kahneman & Tversky 1979; Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994): duration and cost estimates made from the inside view (the plan, the intended steps) are systematically optimistic, even when the estimator knows the relevant base rates. The standard corrective is **reference-class forecasting** — estimating from the outside view: how long did similar tasks actually take.
- **Error detection needs an independent signal.** The same cues that produced an answer tend to re-certify it on immediate self-review; detection rates rise with (i) a delay between production and review, (ii) a changed representation (different modality, format or level of description), (iii) criteria externalised in advance (checklists fixed before the work is seen), and (iv) a different judge. A monitoring failure then becomes a **control failure** when inflated confidence terminates checking early — "it feels done" is the mechanism behind treating a plan, a dispatch, or a fluent draft as a completion.
